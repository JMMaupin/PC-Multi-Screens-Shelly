The consumption history draws a moving average over each outlet's curve. The screens of a second power strip are switched off again while the PC sleeps, an older copy can no longer damage the configuration, a power strip whose name stopped resolving is found again by its address, and a strip whose measurement froze is restarted by itself.

Illustrated guide: **[jmmaupin.github.io/PC-Multi-Screens-Shelly](https://jmmaupin.github.io/PC-Multi-Screens-Shelly/)**.

## Install

Download `ShellyPCScreens-{version}.exe` and run it: it offers to update the installed copy, and asks for an administrator once. Running instances in every session close by themselves first.

The executable is not signed yet: on first launch, SmartScreen shows *Windows protected your PC* → *More info* → *Run anyway*.

SHA-256: `{sha256}`

## New

- **A moving average over the power curve.** The history window can draw an exponential moving average (*EMA*) over the selected outlet's curve, with a time constant from 1 to 10 minutes, set next to the scale. A working PC jumps between 90 and 450 W every few seconds: the average turns that into a level one can read. It is exact on readings however far apart (five seconds while the PC runs, minutes from the on-device probe while it sleeps), starts over after a gap rather than bridging it, and its value under the pointer is shown next to the measured one. The choice is kept for the next opening.

## Fixes

- **A power strip whose measurement froze is restarted by itself.** The firmware's metering can freeze: the strip keeps answering and switching, but repeats the same figures. On the strip that powers the PC, it froze as the PC was switched on, on a standby 1 W while the PC drew 150: the on-device script never saw the PC start, and no screen came on. The application, which runs on the PC, now takes a PC outlet reading absurd figures (a mains frequency out of 45 to 65 Hz) or figures frozen for five minutes for a fault, and restarts that strip: its relays keep their position, the PC's outlet included. At most once every 30 minutes and three times a day, written to the log and announced by a notification.
- **A frozen measurement no longer puts the PC at no known location.** Its outlet reading next to nothing was taken for a PC elsewhere: profiles turned off, *install the script from the location it is at* when trying to reinstall it. A PC outlet whose measurement gave out now proves nothing, and its location is kept when its strip answers.
- **The device list shows its location even when there is only one.** The devices were grouped under their location from the second one on: with a single location, nothing said where they were, nor that the PC had been recognised there (*the PC is here*). The location row now always heads them, and can be renamed from there (*Location...*).
- **The on-device script reaches the other power strips by their address.** While the PC sleeps or is off, the script on the strip that powers it switches the screens off, those of the other strips included, over HTTP. It reached them by their `.local` name, which the strip's own firmware stopped resolving (*Host not found*), while their address answered at once: their screens stayed on all night, with no trace anywhere, and did not come back on by themselves at wake either. A strip whose address is fixed is now reached at that address; only one on DHCP is still reached by its name. The script is updated by itself at the first start of this version.
- **A strip out of the script's reach is reported.** Every hour, and as soon as the script is installed again, the strip that hosts the script is asked to make the very same request to each other strip (same address, same credentials, same delay, as many attempts), on a method that switches nothing. A strip it cannot reach is written to the log, announced by a notification, and listed in the icon's menu (*On-device script cannot reach strip2*) for as long as it lasts.
- **A strip slow to answer on wake no longer aborts the resume.** When it did not answer at its address yet, the strip was looked for by its name, and Windows could resolve that name to an IPv6 address the network layer refuses (`connect(): flowinfo must be 0-1048575`). That error was not taken for a network failure: it stopped the reconnection of every strip, and the profile was not applied again. It now counts as an unreachable strip, and an unexpected error on one strip no longer prevents the others from being connected.
- **An older copy no longer rewrites the configuration.** Each file now records the version that wrote it (`written_by`). A copy older than the newest of them writes nothing, drives no device, and says so: blue icon, *Version 3.2.0, older than its configuration (3.2.0): changes nothing* in its menu, and a notification at start. Before, an old copy still installed, started by Windows while a newer one was being tried, rewrote `state.json` in its own format: the screen layout and the fixed addresses were lost. Only versions from this one on respect the rule: an older copy still installed somewhere can still do the damage, so keep the installed copy up to date.
- **A power strip is asked at its last known address first.** It used to be looked for by its `.local` name, then by a sweep of the network; its address was tried directly only once known to be fixed. A strip whose name stopped resolving, and whose fixed-address mark had been lost, then stayed unfound for hours while its address answered in forty milliseconds. The address is now tried first, kept only if the MAC that answers is the strip's own.
- **The sweep finds strips with the newer firmware.** The Power Strip 4 Gen4 now reports itself as `PowerStripZB`, a name the sweep did not know: it skipped the strip, even when looking for its precise MAC. A known MAC now settles the matter alone, whatever the device reports.
- **A closed window no longer leaves its Tcl interpreter behind.** Each window (profiles, settings, history) has its own, which only its own thread may delete. Once the window was closed, Python's cycle collector freed it later, from whichever thread was running at the time. Depending on the Python the executable is built with, the interpreter was then either left in memory for good, or the whole application crashed with no trace in its log, a few minutes after the window closed or while quitting. Each window now deletes its interpreter itself, as it closes.

Version numbers now always have three parts: `major.minor.fix`.
